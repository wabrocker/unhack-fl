About working the polls

What the job actually is. Poll workers run the polling place on election day: setting up equipment before doors open, checking voters in, issuing ballots, helping people who have questions or need assistance, and closing the site down at the end of the night. Roles differ — a clerk runs the location, inspectors handle check-in and ballots.

It's a full day. You start before polls open and finish after they close, so expect a long one rather than a shift you can fit around other plans.

Who can do it. Reported requirements are US citizenship, Florida residency, and being at least 16 — 18 to serve as an inspector. Training is required beforehand.

Why it counts as common ground. Every county needs workers and both parties need them staffed. It's the least contested civic job in the state.

Specifics vary by county and the details above still need checking against current law. Your county's elections office is the authority.

About public records requests

What Chapter 119 is. Florida's public records law — among the broadest in the country, and backed by a right of access written into the state constitution (Art. I, §24). The working assumption is that government records belong to the public unless a specific exemption says otherwise.

How fast they must answer. Chapter 119 sets no express deadline. An agency gets a reasonable time to locate the records, review them, and redact anything exempt — how long that is depends on how big and scattered the request is.

Questions people actually ask:

  • How much did the city spend on outside legal counsel in 2025, and which firms were paid?
  • The current contract between the school district and its transportation vendor.
  • Emails between the county commission and a named developer about a specific project, over a specific few months.
  • Inspection reports for a particular building or facility.

What makes a request work. Name the records you want, not the conclusion you're after — a date range and a department turn a vague ask into one a clerk can actually search for.

Draft a records request

This helps the people who answer it, too

A records request lands on the desk of a public employee — a clerk, a records custodian, an administrator. Around 23 million Americans work for federal, state and local government, and almost none of them are politicians (BLS: roughly 15.2M local, 5.5M state, 2.7M federal).

They are already of the people, by the people, and for the people. The goal here is not to cut them back or replace them. Their experience is the asset — decades of knowing how things actually work — and we want it kept and put to better use.

A clear request is a kindness. A vague one can cost someone hours of guessing at what you meant and searching the wrong systems. Naming the record, the office and the dates turns that into a search they can run. That's the whole reason this page exists: not to generate more mail, but to make the mail that gets sent easier to answer.

Not sure what to ask for? Pick what you care about:

The office that made or keeps the record — not the county in general.

  • School spending or board minutes → the school board
  • Incident or arrest reports → the sheriff, or a city police department
  • Council votes, contracts, agendas → the city or county clerk
  • Permits and inspections → the building department

Not sure? Send it to your best guess. Offices routinely forward misdirected requests, and asking the wrong one costs you time rather than the right to ask.

Name the record, not the conclusion. Ask for the invoices, not for "evidence of waste" — a clerk can search for invoices.

Give a date range, even a rough one. It is the single thing that most reduces the work on both ends.

Works: "Invoices from outside law firms paid by the city between January 1 and December 31, 2025."

Too vague: "Everything about legal spending."
